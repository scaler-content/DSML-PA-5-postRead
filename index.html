Step 1 · Clarify the problem

The Problem Statement

Uber is getting complaints about ride cancellations by drivers and non-availability of cars on a specific route. The uneven supply-demand gap for cabs City ↔ Airport is hurting customer relationships and losing revenue.

Aim: identify the root cause (cancellations + non-availability) and recommend ways to tackle it.

Quiz 1 · Feature engineering — defining supply
The new "Cab Availability" column labels a request "Available" only when Status == "Trip Completed," and "Not Available" for everything else (Cancelled, No Cars Available). Why is this the right way to model supply vs demand?
Answer: B. Supply is only "real" when a rider actually got a completed trip. Cancellations and "No Cars Available" are demand that went unmet — exactly the gap we're investigating. Collapsing status into Available vs Not Available turns raw outcomes into a clean supply-demand signal (42% vs 58%).

Quiz 2 · Reading the demand-supply gap
The data shows heavy "No Cars Available" from Airport → City in the evening, and heavy "Cancellations" from City → Airport in the morning. What single mechanism best explains BOTH?
Answer: B. It's a positioning problem. Because few trips completed in the previous hours, cars aren't where the next demand spike is — so the evening airport surge sees "No Cars Available," and the morning city surge sees drivers cancelling long, awkward airport pickups. One root mechanism, two symptoms.

Step 6 · The mechanism, named

Airport → City · 5–9 PM

High demand, low supply due to 'No Cars Available' — because in the previous hours fewer people travelled City → Airport, so few cars are near the Airport.

City → Airport · 5–9 AM

High demand, low supply due to Ride Cancellations — few airport trips completed earlier, so cabs must come from far (City), then wait for the passenger, so drivers cancel.

🤖 How can AI help here?

Once the charts show when and where the gap happens, AI can help find the why. Give it the observations and ask for ranked hypotheses:

"Airport→City has heavy 'No Cars Available' between 5–9 PM. City→Airport has heavy driver cancellations between 5–9 AM. Suggest 3 likely reasons that explain both, ranked."
AI usually returns something like
  • Cabs are stranded at the wrong end of the route, because few trips ran that way in the previous hours
  • Drivers avoid long, low-earning airport pickups that come with waiting time
  • Demand peaks (flight timings) don't line up with driver shift patterns

Your job as the analyst: keep only the hypotheses the data supports. Here, the hourly route charts confirm #1 and #2. Treat AI output as a list of hypotheses to test, not as the answer.

Step 7 · Recommendations

Move the Supply to Meet the Demand

Waiting incentive

Reward drivers for waiting at the Airport.

Night-shift pay

Compensate drivers for the 00:00–05:00 slot.

Pre-position

Proactively place a few cabs at the Airport ahead of the surge.

Airport-ride reward

Reward airport rides to make up for the lost time.

Wider discovery

Increase the cab-discovery range at the Airport for a wider search.

Quiz 3 · Fix the root cause, not the symptom
Given the root mechanism (cabs stranded at the wrong end of the airport route), which recommendation most directly attacks the cause rather than a symptom?
Answer: B. The gap exists because cars aren't positioned where demand will spike. Waiting incentives, airport-ride rewards and proactive pre-positioning move supply to meet demand — fixing the cause. A prettier error message or hiding the destination only masks the symptom.

Quiz 4 · Direct cause or root cause?
A factory ships malfunctioning products. Investigation finds a faulty component from a supplier whose quality-control process is weak. In RCA terms, which is the root cause?
Answer: C. The malfunction is the symptom and the faulty component is the direct cause — both are downstream and observable. The root cause is the supplier's inadequate quality control: fix that and you stop future faulty components (and malfunctions) at the source.

Practice Questions

Frame your own answer first — then reveal the approach.

Q1 — A ride app sees high cancellations on one route. How would you investigate with data?
Data-driven RCA
Clarify: define cancellation rate; split by route (intercity vs airport); list Internal/External/Segmentation hypotheses.
Engineer: hour, time slot, and a clean supply signal (completed = available, else unmet demand).
Explore: gap by hour × pickup point × time slot to locate the peaks.
Diagnose: name the mechanism (supply positioned at the wrong end), then recommend positioning fixes.
Q2 — Distinguish a direct cause from a root cause with an example.
Concept
Direct: the faulty component that failed — visible, immediate, short-term.
Root: the supplier's weak QC that let it through — hidden, underlying, long-term.
Test: fixing the root prevents recurrence; fixing the direct cause only mops the puddle.
Q3 — Your orders dipped but nothing changed internally. Where do you look?
External step
Competitor analysis: did a rival launch a sale, a new tier or a better offer?
Other external: holidays, strikes, controversy, seasonality, macro shifts.
A metric can dip because a rival moved — not because you broke something. Know the competitor cold.
Q4 — Why engineer a "time slot" feature instead of just using the raw hour?
Feature engineering
Raw hours are noisy (24 thin bars); grouping into Dawn / Early Morning / Noon / Late Evening / Night makes the pattern legible.
It surfaced the two worst slots (Late Evening & Early Morning) cleanly — the insight was in the grouping, not the raw hour.

Key Takeaways

1
RCA on data is still CRIED

Clarify + hypotheses → rule out (nulls, dupes, bugs) → explore → diagnose. Every hypothesis is now testable against the data.

2
Feature engineering makes the pattern visible

Hour → time slot, and a clean supply signal (completed = available). The insight lived in the grouping — 58% of requests went unmet.

3
Two symptoms, one mechanism

Evening "No Cars" (Airport→City) and morning "Cancellations" (City→Airport) are both cabs stranded at the wrong end of the route.

4
Fix the cause, not the symptom

Pre-position cabs and incentivise waiting / airport rides — move supply to where demand will spike, don't just reword the error.

5
Direct ≠ root; competitors are External

Fix the root to prevent recurrence, and remember a rival's move can move your metric — competitor analysis is part of RCA.
